What Is an HTS Code?

HTS stands for Harmonized Tariff Schedule. In the United States, HTS codes, also called HTSUS codes, classify imported goods. A complete U.S. HTS statistical reporting number contains ten digits and builds on the internationally standardized six-digit Harmonized System (HS).

HTS codes help customs officials identify a product’s classification. Together with details such as country of origin and customs value, they support duty assessment, the application of trade measures and import statistics. The U.S. International Trade Commission publishes the HTSUS; U.S. Customs and Border Protection administers it at importation.

A U.S. HTS code has ten digits. Digits 1 and 2 are the chapter, 3 and 4 the heading, 5 and 6 the subheading, and 7 to 10 the U.S. tariff and statistical suffix.

The broad product family. Chapter 01 covers live animals; 61 covers apparel.

A narrower group inside the chapter. 0101 covers live horses, asses, mules and hinnies; 6109 covers T-shirts.

Digits 5–6

Subheading

The six-digit level shared by every country. 010121 narrows 0101 to pure-bred breeding horses.

Digits 7–10

U.S. HTS suffix

The HTSUS closes the legal line at eight digits, then adds a two-digit statistical suffix. Together they form the ten-digit HTS code, and each line carries its own General, Special and Column 2 duty rate.

HS Code vs. HTS Code vs. Schedule B

HS codes, HTS codes and Schedule B numbers all classify goods, but they serve different purposes. The HS provides the international framework, the U.S. HTS classifies imports, and Schedule B is used for U.S. export statistics.

FeatureHS codeU.S. HTS codeSchedule B

The final four digits of an HTS code and a Schedule B number can differ, so they should not be treated as interchangeable. A supplier’s six-digit HS code can start your search, but the full code required for a U.S. import declaration still has to be verified.

How to Determine the HTS Code for Your Product

Determining an HTS code involves more than searching for a product name. The classification depends on the product’s characteristics, the tariff language and the applicable rules.

  1. 1

    Gather the product details

    Identify the material, composition, primary function and relevant technical specifications. Establish whether it is a finished product, an unfinished item, a component, or part of a set.

  2. 2

    Compare potential classifications

    Use the lookup to find relevant entries, then compare the descriptions of possible headings and subheadings. Do not select the first result simply because its wording sounds similar.

  3. 3

    Review the classification rules and legal notes

    Apply the General Rules of Interpretation and review the relevant Section and Chapter Notes. Products with similar names can still require different classifications.

  4. 4

    Confirm the full ten-digit code

    Follow the hierarchy: the first four digits identify the heading, the first six the HS subheading, the first eight the U.S. tariff provision, and the final two the statistical suffix.

  5. 5

    Resolve any uncertainty

    Check the current HTSUS and relevant CBP classification rulings. For complex or uncertain classifications, consult a customs broker or consider requesting a binding ruling from CBP.

Common Mistakes to Avoid During an HTS Code Search

Finding a relevant code does not necessarily mean you have found the correct classification.

Common mistakeHow to avoid it
Choosing a code based only on the product nameCheck the material, function, specifications and applicable legal notes, not just matching keywords.
Using a six-digit HS code as the complete U.S. reporting numberVerify the full ten-digit HTS statistical reporting number.
Copying a supplier’s code without checking itConfirm that the code matches the actual product and the U.S. import classification system.
Treating Schedule B and HTS codes as interchangeableDistinguish U.S. export statistical reporting from import declaration requirements.
Reusing an outdated codeCheck the current schedule for changes to codes, statistical suffixes and tariff provisions.
Assuming the base duty rate is the total import chargeReview origin, customs value, preferential treatment and any additional duties or trade measures.

These checks reduce classification and reporting errors, but they do not replace a product-specific review of the current requirements.

HTS Code Lookup FAQs

Classification questions that come up before a U.S. import declaration.

Are HTS codes universal?

No. U.S. HTS codes are specific to U.S. imports. Their first six digits follow the international HS framework, but the additional U.S. subdivisions are not universal. Other countries may require a different complete declaration code.

How many digits are in an HTS code?

A complete U.S. HTS statistical reporting number has ten digits. The first six identify the international HS classification, digits seven and eight identify the U.S. tariff subdivision, and the final two form the statistical suffix. Shorter numbers in the schedule represent broader classification levels.

How many HTS codes are there?

The U.S. HTS contains tens of thousands of classification entries. The exact count depends on the schedule revision and on whether you count eight-digit tariff provisions or ten-digit statistical reporting numbers. There is no single fixed total that applies to every edition.

What is the difference between an ECCN and an HTS code?

An HTS code classifies imported goods for tariff and statistical purposes. An Export Control Classification Number is a five-character alphanumeric designation used to identify items on the U.S. Commerce Control List for export-control purposes. The two systems are not interchangeable. An HTS code does not establish a product’s ECCN or, by itself, determine whether an export license is required.

Does an HTS code tell me how much import duty I will pay?

Not by itself. An HTS code helps identify the applicable tariff provision, but the final amount can also depend on customs value, country of origin, preferential treatment and additional duties. The base duty rate shown in a lookup result may not reflect the total import cost.

How often are HTS codes updated?

The HTSUS can be revised multiple times within a year. Before reusing a previously assigned code, check the current edition for changes to the classification, statistical suffix or applicable tariff provisions.

Who is responsible for choosing the correct HTS code?

The importer of record is responsible for exercising reasonable care when declaring a product’s classification, even when using a customs broker. CBP may review the declaration and determine whether the classification is correct.
